Design is part of the solution to building an application, and so is being able to explain it. These are the practices I lean on to reason about a design, argue for a redesign, and know when not to.

## Customer conversations: the Mom Test

<Lede>Learn from people's past behaviour, not their opinions of your idea.</Lede>

Before structure or screens, I talk to the people who have the problem, following Rob Fitzpatrick's [The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/). Ask someone whether your idea is good and they'll be kind, the way your mom would. So I don't pitch. I ask about their life:

- Talk about their life instead of your idea: what they do today, and where it hurts
- Ask about specifics in the past, like "when did this last happen, and what did you do?", instead of "would you use this?"
- Talk less and listen more

Compliments and "I'd definitely use that" are bad data. What people already did, what it cost them, and what they'll commit to now are good data. Those answers decide what gets designed, and they're what I point to when I argue for a redesign, or against one. I've written more about it on my [about page](/about), and there's a worked example in the [Scoreboard](/blog/scoreboard) post.

## Information architecture

<Lede>Clear structure and user flows so people find what they need fast.</Lede>

A clear information architecture reduces frustration and lifts satisfaction, and that is what creates engagement. Get the structure right before the screens.

## Wireframing

<Lede>Blueprints for layout and function, so work moves even when customer feedback is slow.</Lede>

Wireframes let you keep moving on structure while waiting on feedback, surface issues early, and give users and stakeholders something concrete to react to. They save time and money before pixels are spent.
