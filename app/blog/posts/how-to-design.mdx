Design is part of the solution, and so is being able to explain it. These are the practices I use to reason about a design, argue for a redesign, and know when not to.

## Customer conversations: the Mom Test

<Lede>Learn from people's past behaviour, not their opinions of your idea.</Lede>

Ask someone whether your idea is good and they'll be kind, the way your mom would. So, following Rob Fitzpatrick's [The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/), I don't pitch:

- Talk about their life, not your idea
- Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future
- Talk less and listen more

What people already did, and what they'll commit to now, decides what gets designed. There's a worked example in the [Scoreboard](/blog/scoreboard) post.

## Intuition: when to go, and when not to

<Lede>Good design makes the next step feel obvious, and makes the risky step feel like a step.</Lede>

- **Go** looks like go: one clear primary action, labelled with what it does.
- **Not yet** looks like not yet: a disabled button that says what's missing.
- **Stop** costs a moment, saved for deleting, paying, or sending to everyone.
- **Undo** beats "Are you sure?".
- **Feedback** answers every tap.

The same signals apply to me as the designer. I decide on the spot when a change is cheap to undo and a pattern I've seen work. I stop and check when it's hard to reverse, touches a lot of people, or is new territory. When instinct and evidence disagree, that's usually the most useful thing I learn.

## Information architecture

<Lede>Clear structure and user flows so people find what they need fast.</Lede>

Get the structure right before the screens.

## Wireframing

<Lede>Blueprints for layout and function, so work moves even when customer feedback is slow.</Lede>

Wireframes surface issues early and give people something concrete to react to, before pixels are spent.
